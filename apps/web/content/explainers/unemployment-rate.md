## Short answer

The unemployment rate is the number of unemployed people divided by the labor force. A person is unemployed when they have no job, are available to work, and looked for work in the past four weeks, or are on temporary layoff. The labor force is everyone working plus everyone unemployed.

For counties and states, the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes these figures through its Local Area Unemployment Statistics program, which combines survey responses, payroll records, and unemployment-insurance claims in statistical models. Local rates are estimates, and they are revised.

## What it is not

- It is not the share of all adults without a job. Retirees, full-time students, and people who stopped looking are outside the labor force and are not counted at all.
- It is not a measure of underemployment. Someone working a few hours a week who wants full-time work counts as employed.
- It is not the number of jobs in a place. That comes from a different survey; see "Why do jobs and employed people differ?".

## Worked example

A falling rate can mean more people found work, or that people who were looking gave up and left the labor force. Read the rate beside the size of the labor force before deciding which happened.
