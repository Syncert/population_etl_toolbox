Place Almanac Plan

A website that tells an individual about their county, their state, and the nation, drawn from the seven public sources already in the warehouse, and shaped so every page is also a YouTube segment.

Drafted 2026-10-06Grounded in the repo's current sources, grains, and web routesNumbers in wireframes are placeholders, never data

Where you are, and why the website feels stuck

The warehouse is further along than the website framing. Seven sources are captured, conformed, and published with provenance. A versioned API serves them. The Next.js app has catalog, explorer, compare, workbench, quality, builder, article, and twenty use-case pages.

What exists is organized the way an analyst thinks: by tool (explore, compare, workbench) and by professional use case (grant needs assessment, workforce briefing). That is the right design for the people in the top-20 document. It is the wrong front door for an individual. A resident does not arrive with a use case. They arrive with a place and a question: "What is going on in my county, and is that normal?"

The pivot. Organize the public site by place, not by tool or use case. Give every county, state, and the nation one page with the same chapters in the same order. Draw every number three times, county beside state beside nation, so "is that normal" is answered by the picture itself.

The existing tools do not go away. They become the "dig deeper" layer one click below every chart.

What kind of website

Three patterns were on the table. A dashboard portal (filters, dropdowns, a map) is what the app already is and what residents bounce off. A news site (articles first) depends on a publishing cadence you have not built yet and buries the evergreen value. An almanac wins: a reference page per place that is always true as of its stated periods, with an editorial layer on top for what changed.

Think of it as a Wikipedia article for every county, written entirely from numbers you can trace, with a monthly briefing attached. That shape has three properties the data supports and a YouTube channel needs:

  • Nested levels on every chart. County, state, and nation are the three grains every source except FRED publishes, and the geography master data already holds the containment. One visual grammar serves all 3,200 counties.
  • Same chapters everywhere. People, Work and Money, Housing, Health, Safety, Land and Farms, Change. Each chapter maps to a source bundle you already publish. A fixed chapter order is also a fixed episode rundown.
  • Every number carries its period, source, and caveat. The repo's guardrails (no composite scores, suppressed is not zero, association is not causation) become the site's voice rather than fine print. That voice is the channel's credibility.

The pages, with wireframes and why each exists

Each wireframe is desktop width. Every page stacks to one column at phone width, and every chart keeps a table alternative, which the app already guarantees.

1. Find your place

/
Why it exists

An individual's first question is "where is mine". The home page does exactly one thing: get them to their county page in one action. The map is navigation, not analysis.

What it draws from

Geography master data for search and the county map tiles already served by Martin. Three featured places rotate from the briefing.

YouTube use

The channel trailer and every episode's call to action: "find yours at the link". The national map with one measure painted is a recurring cold-open frame.

2. Place page

/us/<state>/<county>, /us/<state>, /us
Why it exists

This is the product. One permanent, linkable page per place that reads top to bottom like an almanac entry. Seven chapters in a fixed order: People, Work and Money, Housing, Health, Safety, Land and Farms, Change over time. Each chapter opens with two or three headline cards drawn three times (county, state, nation), then one trend chart with the state and nation as reference lines, then a footnote naming period, source, and caveat, and a "dig deeper" link into the explorer with the exact query.

What it draws from

The eight reviewed profile templates and the use-case sections already compose these measures; this page re-arranges them by place instead of by use case. ACS and PEP fill People, Housing, and Change. LAUS and ACS fill Work and Money, with CPI and FRED shown as a clearly labeled national backdrop. PLACES fills Health at county grain and CDI at state. FBI fills Safety at state until the county roll-up lands. NASS fills Land and Farms where the county has published cells, and the chapter is omitted rather than shown empty where it has none.

YouTube use

The page is the episode. The chapter order is the rundown, each headline card is a scene, and the trend chart is the b-roll. A series like "Your county in seven charts" can be produced for any county with the same template, which is what makes a channel sustainable.

3. Compare two places

/us/wi/dane/vs/us/wi/brown
Why it exists

"Is that normal" has a second answer: "compared to where". Residents compare their county to the one they moved from, the one next door, or the one in the news. The page keeps the same chapter order as the place page, so it is the place page folded in half.

What it draws from

The existing comparison workspace's preflight rules decide which measures are comparable (same source, same period, same grain). The page only offers pairs that pass, and says why a measure is missing rather than hiding it.

YouTube use

A "two counties" format: pick two places with a story between them and walk the chapters. Easy to script, naturally visual, and it drives traffic to both place pages.

4. Explainer

/explain/<concept>
Why it exists

Every caveat the warehouse preserves needs one plain-language home, written once and linked from everywhere. The first dozen write themselves from the guardrails: what the unemployment rate counts, why a margin of error exists, PEP estimates versus ACS surveys, why missing crime reports are not zero, why the CPI is not your local cost of living, what a suppressed cell means, why modeled health prevalence is not a case count.

What it draws from

The reviewed semantic definitions directory, which already requires intended use, limitations, and citations per metric. The worked example pulls the reader's last-viewed place so the concept lands on their own number.

YouTube use

Evergreen "one number explained" videos, three to four minutes each. The explainer's short answer is the script, the diagram is the key frame, and the worked example is the payoff.

5. One measure, every county

/map/<measure>
Why it exists

People love rankings, and rankings are where public-data sites lose their integrity. This page allows exactly one measure at a time, states its period and denominator, shows uncertainty where the source has it, and counts the counties with no published value instead of painting them. No composite index, ever, which the top-20 guardrails already demand.

What it draws from

The explorer's map wiring and the rule that a geography without a value is left uncoloured. The ranked table is the same observations as the map, which the accessibility commitments already require.

YouTube use

"Where is it highest, where is it lowest" episodes, one measure each. The map animates as the cold open; the top and bottom ten are the body; the coverage note is the honest close.

6. Monthly briefing

/briefing/<yyyy-mm>
Why it exists

The almanac is evergreen; the briefing is the reason to come back. Each month, which releases landed (LAUS, CPI, PEP vintage, ACS release, PLACES release), what moved, and which places it touched. It is written from evidence blocks, never from prose alone, so every claim is reproducible.

What it draws from

The evidence packet builder and the article reader already exist. What is missing is the approval path from private to published, which is the publishing plan in the backlog. The briefing is the first consumer of that path and the reason to build it.

YouTube use

The monthly episode. The briefing's blocks are the segments, in order. Publish the page and the video the same day; each links to the other.

7. Where the numbers come from

/data
Why it exists

Trust is the product's only moat. A public, plain-language version of the quality explorer: when each source last refreshed, what geography it covers, what has been revised, and the five rules the site follows. This is the page a skeptical commenter gets linked to.

What it draws from

The quality explorer and the content-aware health resource. The rules are the top-20 guardrails, reworded for a reader.

YouTube use

A "how this channel works" video and the standing pinned comment. It also pre-empts the most common objections in comments.

8. Studio (operator only)

/studio
Why it exists

This is what makes "easy to prepare YouTube materials" true rather than aspirational. Pick any place, chapter, and card. The studio renders it at 1920 by 1080 and 1080 by 1920 with large type, the three level colours, and the period, source, and caveat line baked into the frame so a clipped screenshot still carries its provenance. A script notes panel assembles the reviewed definition, the caveat, and the exact request so you read it rather than write it.

What it draws from

The same chart components as the place page, in a print-sized layout. Export uses the app's existing CSV and chart export paths plus a frame renderer. Behind the existing bearer credential, since it is not a public surface.

YouTube use

Every frame in every format above comes out of this page. A consistent frame design is what makes a channel recognizable in a feed.

Build order against the repo

Most of the plan is rearrangement of what exists. The genuinely new work is place routing, explainer content, the measure-map page, and the studio renderer. Two backlog plans are upstream dependencies and should be sequenced, not bypassed.

Phase 1
Place pages for counties, states, and the nation. Route by geography identifier, compose chapters from the existing profile and use-case sections, add the three-level card and the reference-line trend. Safety shows the state with a label until the county crime roll-up plan is done. Land and Farms is omitted where NASS has no county cells.
Phase 2
Explainers and the studio. Write the first twelve explainers from the guardrails and the semantic definitions. Build the studio as an authenticated page over the same chart components with 16:9 and 9:16 frame layouts. This is when the channel can start.
Phase 3
Briefings. Implement the publishing approval path plan, then the briefing page as its first consumer. Monthly episodes begin.
Phase 4
Measure maps and compare. The map page over the explorer's wiring with the uncoloured-is-not-zero rule, and the two-place compare over the preflight rules. Cities and towns follow once ACS is ingested at place grain.

Making county and place pages deeper than state and nation

Readers will spend most of their time at county and place grain, so those pages should be the richest, not the thinnest. There are four ways to get there, in rising order of cost. The first two need no new source at all.

1. Use what is already ingested and not yet shown

The ACS configuration pulls roughly fifty tables at county grain, and the current profile templates use a handful. The unused tables are exactly the ones residents ask about: how people get to work and how long it takes, who has broadband, who has health insurance, veterans, who moved in during the last year, where people were born, how old the housing stock is and what kind it is, how many vehicles a household has, which industries and occupations employ people, the full income distribution, inequality, and marital and living-alone status. PEP's components of change (births, deaths, domestic and international migration) answer "why is the population changing" rather than just "is it". All of the released PLACES measures are already offered for counties. This is a chapter-depth change, not a pipeline change.

2. Use the geography master data as content

The versioned geography layer already holds nation, state, county, and place identities, boundaries, and the relationship bridge between them. That is page content waiting to be rendered: the cities and towns that overlap a county, with the note that a place can cross county lines (the contract models places as siblings of counties, not children); the neighbouring counties; the metro or micro area the county belongs to; land area; and the county's rural-urban classification once the USDA ERS codes are added as a small reference table. "Nearby and related places" is the section that turns a page into a browsable almanac, and it also gives the Compare page a sensible default pair.

Two things to keep off the ingest list. Third-party composite rankings (county health rankings, livability indexes, commercial home-value estimates) can be linked as context but not ingested as facts, because they are the unexplained scores the guardrails forbid. And any derived number the site wants, population density from land area, a rate from a count and a denominator, a deviation from the state, belongs in a reviewed derived product like the existing time aggregates, labeled as derived, never computed in the page.

4. Go below the county

The largest step is sub-county geography. ACS publishes its 5-year tables for every tract and block group, and the Census ZIP Code Tabulation Areas sit beside them. With tracts in the geography master data, a county page gains a "within the county" map for any measure, and a place page can show its own neighbourhoods. The volume is roughly twenty-five times the county row count per table, so this is a phase of its own, after place grain, and it is where the versioned boundary design in the geography layer earns its keep.

What this does to the page

Two chapters are added where the sources exist, Schools and Land and Environment, and the chapter list for a county page becomes People, Work and Money, Housing, Schools, Health, Safety, Land and Environment (absorbing Land and Farms), and Change. A county page also gains a Nearby section from the geography data and a "What makes this place distinctive" section listing the handful of measures where the county differs most from its state, each shown separately with its period and uncertainty, never summed. State and national pages keep the same chapters with fewer cards, which is the right asymmetry: the higher levels exist mostly as the reference lines on the county's charts.

Decisions only you can make

  • A name. The almanac framing wants a name a neighbour would say out loud. The repository name is not it.
  • Tone. Reference-grade and calm, or curious and conversational. The data page rules read the same either way; the chapter intros and voice-over do not.
  • Which county first. The first episode and the first polished place page should be a county you know and can talk about. Wisconsin is already the reviewed FBI agency sample, which makes a Wisconsin county a practical start.
  • Whether to promise cities. Places exist in the geography master data and in some PEP releases, but ACS is not ingested below county. Launch with counties and say so, or add place-grain ACS to the backlog first.
